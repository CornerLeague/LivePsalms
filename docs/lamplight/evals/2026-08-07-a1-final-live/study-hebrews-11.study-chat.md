## Reply

Hebrews 11:1 is less a complete dictionary definition than a description of what faith does: it gives present solidity to God’s promised future and conviction concerning realities beyond sight. Jamieson reads “assurance” as faith making promised realities present to the believer; the Geneva Notes similarly say faith sets invisible things “before our eyes.” This fits the lexicon’s range for *pistis*: persuasion, moral conviction, reliance, and constancy.

The chapter then tests that description through examples. Each person acts in the present because God’s unseen word is treated as more substantial than visible circumstances. Noah builds because of an unseen judgment; Abraham travels without knowing his destination; Sarah considers the Promiser faithful despite bodily impossibility; Moses relinquishes Egypt’s visible treasures because he looks toward an unseen reward (Hebrews 11:7–12, 24–27). Faith is therefore not imagination or confidence in oneself. Its object is God—His existence, character, promise, and reward (Hebrews 11:6).

The decisive feature is that faith does not guarantee immediate visible success. Some conquer kingdoms and escape death, while others are imprisoned, tortured, displaced, and killed (Hebrews 11:33–38). Both groups are commended. The passage turns not on outcomes but on fidelity to God amid outcomes. Faith may receive a promise within history, or greet it “from afar” and die still waiting (Hebrews 11:13, 39–40).

That is why “assurance” should not be mistaken for certainty that one’s preferred circumstances will occur. Calvin highlights the chapter’s tension: resurrection is promised while believers remain mortal; blessedness is announced amid misery. Faith holds to the Lord’s truthfulness while sight has not yet caught up. As the parallel texts put it, Christian life proceeds “by faith, not by sight,” fixing attention on what is unseen and eternal (2 Corinthians 5:7; 2 Corinthians 4:18).

In short, Hebrews 11 defines faith as trust in God’s unseen reality that becomes visible through persevering allegiance—whether the faithful person sees fulfillment now or only welcomes it from afar (Hebrews 11:1, 13).
