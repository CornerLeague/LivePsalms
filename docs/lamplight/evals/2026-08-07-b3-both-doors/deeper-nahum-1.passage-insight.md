## How to Read This Passage

Nahum 1 is prophetic poetry: a “burden” and “vision,” not detached reflection or neutral political analysis (Nahum 1:1). Calvin reads those labels as marking both the oracle’s grievous subject and its claimed divine origin. Its storms, drying waters, quaking mountains, fire, thorns, and darkness therefore work as concentrated poetic images of the Lord’s sovereign judgment, not as a timetable of meteorological events (Nahum 1:3; Nahum 1:4; Nahum 1:5; Nahum 1:6; Nahum 1:10). The chapter also changes its audience: it addresses Nineveh’s plotting, Judah’s affliction, and finally Judah’s restored worship (Nahum 1:9; Nahum 1:11; Nahum 1:12; Nahum 1:15). Adam Clarke accordingly describes a deliberate movement from God’s character, through Assyria’s overthrow, to freedom and good news for Judah. Read this oracle by holding together its two governing affirmations: the Lord does not acquit guilt, yet He is good and knows those who trust Him (Nahum 1:3; Nahum 1:7).

## Historical & Cultural Setting

Nahum is placed in Judah before Nineveh’s fall in 612 BC, within the period roughly 650–612 BC. Nineveh was the Assyrian capital, and the oracle addresses a world in which Judah knew Assyrian power not as an abstraction but as a yoke and shackles (Nahum 1:13). The promise that the wicked invader would no longer march through Judah would therefore sound like national deliverance from an established imperial threat, not merely private encouragement (Nahum 1:15). Likewise, the command to resume feasts and fulfill vows pictures public worship no longer overshadowed by invasion (Nahum 1:15). Nineveh’s judgment reaches its political continuity, religious symbols, and civic honor: its name will have no future, its idols will be cut off, and its grave prepared (Nahum 1:14). Clarke proposes that redirected waters may stand behind the flood language and notes how water could damage sun-baked brick construction. That is a historical suggestion, not something the chapter itself explains; the text’s plain emphasis is that Nineveh’s end comes under the Lord’s judgment (Nahum 1:8).

## Theological Significance

The chapter’s theology turns on the unity of divine patience, power, justice, and goodness. “Slow to anger” does not mean indifferent to evil: the same sentence says the Lord is great in power and will not leave guilt unpunished (Nahum 1:3). Nor does vengeance describe uncontrolled temper. The lexicon glosses the repeated verb as “avenge or punish,” while the oracle directs that action toward foes who plot against the Lord and impose bondage (Nahum 1:2; Nahum 1:9; Nahum 1:13). Adam Clarke therefore reads the opening as God’s justice and power “tempered by lenity and goodness.” The refuge statement is not an interruption but the other side of the same divine character: the Lord is a stronghold in distress and cares for those who trust Him (Nahum 1:7). This resonates with the firm foundation that “the Lord knows those who are His” (2 Timothy 2:19), and with the confession that the Lord is good to the soul seeking Him (Lamentations 3:25). Judgment and refuge are thus not rival portraits of God; in this oracle, both answer the reality of oppressive evil.

## Read With Care

Reading the cosmic imagery as a coded chronology misses the genre: whirlwind, melting hills, shattered rocks, flood, thorns, and stubble magnify the Lord’s irresistible judgment (Nahum 1:3; Nahum 1:5; Nahum 1:6; Nahum 1:8; Nahum 1:10). The flood may invite historical reconstruction, and Clarke offers one, but the chapter does not identify the military mechanism. It is also misleading to detach “the LORD is good” from the surrounding judgment, or vengeance from His slowness to anger (Nahum 1:2; Nahum 1:3; Nahum 1:7). Finally, promises made to Judah under Assyrian oppression should not be converted without argument into guarantees that every present distress will end immediately (Nahum 1:12; Nahum 1:13).
