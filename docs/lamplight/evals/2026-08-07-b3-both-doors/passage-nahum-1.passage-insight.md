## Overview

Nahum 1 announces judgment on Nineveh, but its center is the character of the LORD. He is neither impulsive nor indifferent: He is “slow to anger and great in power,” yet He does not treat guilt as harmless (Nahum 1:3). The storm, sea, mountains, and earth imagery presents creation itself yielding before Him (Nahum 1:3–6). The decisive turn comes with “The LORD is good”: the same holy presence that overwhelms His enemies becomes a stronghold for those who trust Him (Nahum 1:7–8). Judah’s good news is therefore liberation, not merely Nineveh’s collapse. The oppressor’s yoke and shackles will be broken, allowing worship to resume without the invader’s threat (Nahum 1:12–15). Divine judgment and refuge are held together in one Lord.

## In the Chapter

Because this passage is the book’s opening chapter, its title immediately identifies what follows as Nahum’s vision and a burden directed against Nineveh (Nahum 1:1). Before describing the city’s fall in detail, the chapter establishes why that fall belongs within the LORD’s justice rather than mere political reversal. Nineveh plots against Him, produces a counselor of wickedness, and trusts in numbers and alliances; the LORD answers that these powers will pass away (Nahum 1:9–12). Judah, meanwhile, has known affliction under that same oppressive power, but is promised release from its yoke (Nahum 1:12–13). Adam Clarke reads the chapter as a deliberate prelude: the LORD’s justice, power, patience, and goodness are introduced first; Assyria’s overthrow and Judah’s freedom follow; then the book turns toward the siege itself. That sequence matters. The military events are interpreted through the character and purpose of God, while the chapter lands not on spectacle but on peace, restored feasts, and fulfilled vows (Nahum 1:15).

## The Chapter's Shape

The chapter moves in four clear stages. First comes a majestic portrait of the LORD: jealous for what is His, avenging evil, patient, powerful, and sovereign over creation (Nahum 1:2–6). Second, a brief hinge distinguishes those who trust Him from those who oppose Him. He knows and shelters the former, while the latter are pursued into darkness (Nahum 1:7–8). This contrast resembles the later affirmation that “the Lord knows those who are His,” while those who name Him must turn from evil (2 Timothy 2:19). Third, the oracle addresses Nineveh’s schemes and announces their end: numerical strength cannot preserve the city, its idols, dynasty, or reputation (Nahum 1:9–14). Finally, the camera turns toward Judah’s mountains. A messenger approaches with good news of peace, and fear gives way to worship (Nahum 1:15). Clarke similarly sees the poem opening with God’s character, turning to Assyria’s overthrow, proclaiming Judah’s release, and concluding with glad tidings.

## Reflection & Application

Nahum refuses two distortions of the Lord: imagining goodness without justice, or power without patient care. The passage turns on the contrast between the enemies He confronts and the people for whom He is a stronghold (Nahum 1:3, 7–8). Read personally, this does not make every hardship easy to classify; it does make trust different from denial. Refuge rests in who the Lord is while distress remains real. Lamentations echoes that goodness toward the seeking soul (Lamentations 3:25), while Psalm 91 pictures nearness to God as dwelling beneath His shelter (Psalms 91:1). Spurgeon reads that dwelling as abiding fellowship rather than an occasional retreat. Nahum’s closing image then joins rescue to renewed worship: peace creates room for feasts, vows, and grateful faithfulness (Nahum 1:15).
