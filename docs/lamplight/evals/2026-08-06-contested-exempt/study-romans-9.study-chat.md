## Reply

Romans 9 plainly teaches that God’s choice precedes human works: Jacob was chosen over Esau “before the twins were born or had done anything good or bad,” so that God’s purpose would rest “not by works but by Him who calls” (rom 9:11–12). The chapter also says mercy “does not depend on man’s desire or effort” (rom 9:16). Reformed Christians therefore read this as teaching the unconditional election of particular individuals to salvation.

But whether *individual eternal salvation* is the chapter’s sole or immediate subject is disputed among orthodox Christians. Paul begins with Israel’s apparent unbelief and asks whether God’s covenant word has failed (rom 9:1–6). Isaac and Jacob are individuals, yet they also represent covenant lines and peoples; the argument eventually concerns Jews and Gentiles being called into God’s people (rom 9:24–26). On this reading, many Wesleyan/Arminian interpreters emphasize God’s sovereign choice of the covenant people and the historical roles through which the promise advances, while holding that individuals participate through faith. Paul’s conclusion explicitly contrasts righteousness received “by faith” with pursuit “as if it were by works” (rom 9:30–33).

The hardest language—the potter and vessels of mercy or wrath—supports a strong claim about God’s freedom and initiative (rom 9:20–24). Yet Paul also describes God as bearing the vessels of wrath “with great patience,” and he closes by placing real weight upon believing or stumbling (rom 9:22, 32–33).

So the careful answer is: **Romans 9 can coherently be read as teaching God’s pre-birth choice of individuals for salvation, but the chapter does not settle that interpretation beyond dispute.** It certainly teaches that salvation originates in God’s merciful calling rather than ancestry, merit, desire, or effort (rom 9:6–16). The precise relation between sovereign election and human faith remains disputed among orthodox Christians and is worth discussing within your own church tradition.
