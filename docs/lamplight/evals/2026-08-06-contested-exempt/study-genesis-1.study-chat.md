## Reply

The refrain “God saw that it was good” functions as God’s own evaluation of creation. “Good” here is not merely “pleasant-looking”; within the chapter, it means that what God has made is fitting, ordered, and serving the purpose assigned to it. Light is distinguished from darkness, land from sea, and living things reproduce according to their kinds; after these acts of ordering and filling, God declares them good (Genesis 1:4, 10, 12, 18, 21, 25).

The phrase also establishes that creation is good because it comes from God—not because the created world is divine, but because it faithfully bears the intention of its Maker. The sun and moon, for example, are not named as deities, as they often were in the ancient Near Eastern setting; they are simply created “lights” appointed to govern times and illuminate the earth (Genesis 1:14–18). Their goodness lies in their creaturely place and task. Revelation echoes this God-centered vision: all things exist by the Lord’s will, so creation summons worship of the Creator rather than worship of creation (Revelation 4:11).

The repetition builds toward a deliberate climax. Individual works are repeatedly called “good,” but after humanity is created in God’s image and commissioned to rule within the created order, God sees the whole and calls it “very good” (Genesis 1:26–31). The passage turns on wholeness: each part is good, and the integrated creation is exceedingly fitting.

This also gives Genesis’s later account of sin its proper backdrop. Disorder and corruption are not presented as the Creator’s original workmanship. Genesis 1 begins with darkness and an unformed earth, yet the Spirit hovers, God speaks, boundaries emerge, life flourishes, and emptiness becomes an inhabited world (Genesis 1:2–3; Isaiah 45:18). The refrain therefore teaches both the goodness of embodied creation and its complete dependence upon the Lord’s ordering word.
