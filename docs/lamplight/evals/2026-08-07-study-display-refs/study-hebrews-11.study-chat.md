## Reply

Hebrews 11:1 is less a complete dictionary definition than a description of faith at work. Jamieson, Fausset & Brown reads it in the chapter’s larger setting as faith’s relation to perseverance: faith gives present solidity to what God has promised and conviction concerning realities not yet visible. The lexicon’s *pistis* includes persuasion, conviction, reliance, and constancy—fitting the chapter’s emphasis on enduring trust.

The two halves of the verse reinforce each other. Faith is “the assurance of what we hope for” and “the certainty of what we do not see” (Hebrews 11:1). Hope concerns what has not yet arrived; faith treats God’s trustworthiness as substantial in the present. This does not mean imagining hard enough that something becomes true. Its basis is God’s command, warning, call, and promise: creation comes by God’s word, Noah is warned, Abraham is called, and Sarah judges the Promiser faithful (Hebrews 11:3; Hebrews 11:7–11).

The examples then show this unseen conviction becoming visible allegiance. Abel offers; Noah builds; Abraham leaves without knowing his destination; Moses rejects Egypt’s status; Rahab welcomes the spies (Hebrews 11:4; Hebrews 11:7–8; Hebrews 11:24–26; Hebrews 11:31). Faith is therefore not mere inward optimism. It acts in the present because it relies upon the Lord’s unseen reality and promised future—much like walking “by faith, not by sight” (2 Corinthians 5:7).

Crucially, the chapter does not define faith by favorable outcomes. Some conquer and escape, while others are imprisoned, displaced, and killed (Hebrews 11:33–38). Both groups are commended. The decisive feature is not visible success but enduring reliance: “These were all commended for their faith, yet they did not receive what was promised” (Hebrews 11:39). Abraham and the others welcomed the promises “from afar,” seeking the city God prepares (Hebrews 11:13–16).

So the chapter’s argument turns on this: faith receives God’s unseen promise as trustworthy enough to shape present life, even when fulfillment remains distant. Its final horizon is communal and completed by God—“together with us they would be made perfect” (Hebrews 11:40).
