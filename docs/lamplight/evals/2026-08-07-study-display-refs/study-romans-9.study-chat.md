## Reply

Romans 9 is a major text behind the view that God chooses particular individuals for salvation before birth, but orthodox Christians dispute whether that is Paul’s precise claim here.

**Reformed readings** emphasize Jacob and Esau: before they “had done anything good or bad,” God’s electing purpose stood “not by works but by Him who calls” (Romans 9:11–12). They connect this with God’s freedom to show mercy and with the potter-and-clay image (Romans 9:15–23). On this reading, the individuals illustrate unconditional election to salvation.

**Wesleyan/Arminian and some corporate-election readings** answer that Paul’s controlling question is whether God’s promises to Israel have failed (Romans 9:6), not primarily how every individual is eternally saved. Jacob and Esau represent covenantal lines and peoples; the quotation “Jacob I loved” comes from Malachi’s address concerning Jacob and Esau’s descendants (Romans 9:13; Malachi 1:2). Paul then speaks of Jews and Gentiles being called into God’s people (Romans 9:24–26). On this reading, the chapter chiefly defends God’s freedom to define his covenant people around Christ rather than ancestry or works.

The chapter’s ending is important for both readings. Paul does not conclude with bare fatalism: Gentiles obtained righteousness “by faith,” while Israel stumbled because its pursuit was “not by faith, but as if it were by works” (Romans 9:30–33). The promised foundation is the one who believes in God’s cornerstone (Isaiah 28:16).

So the careful answer is: **Romans 9 can be read as teaching individual, pre-birth election to salvation, and historically many Christians have read it that way; but the chapter does not settle that interpretation beyond dispute.** Its unmistakable claims are that salvation rests on God’s mercy rather than human merit, God’s word has not failed, and belonging to his people is centered on faith in Christ (Romans 9:6, 16, 30–33). This is worth studying with your pastor or church tradition alongside Romans 10–11.
