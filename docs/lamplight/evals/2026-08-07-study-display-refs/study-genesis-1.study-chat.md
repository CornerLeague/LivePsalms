## Reply

The refrain “God saw that it was good” functions as God’s own evaluation of creation. “Good” here is not merely aesthetic—creation is pleasing—but also fitting: each part corresponds to God’s intention and contributes to an ordered, fruitful world. Light and darkness are distinguished, waters receive boundaries, land produces vegetation, and living creatures fill their appointed realms (Genesis 1:4; Genesis 1:10; Genesis 1:12; Genesis 1:18; Genesis 1:21; Genesis 1:25).

The repetition also gives the chapter a rhythm of **speech, fulfillment, and approval**: “God said,” “it was so,” and “God saw that it was good.” Creation therefore is neither accidental nor resistant to its Maker. God’s word effectively brings forth what He wills; Hebrews summarizes this by saying that “the universe was formed at God’s command” (Hebrews 11:3).

Significantly, the refrain is absent after the second day, but the completed arrangement involving the waters and dry land is called good on the third. The text does not explain the omission, so it is best not to build a doctrine from it.

The final evaluation intensifies the refrain: after humanity is created in God’s image and entrusted with rule, God sees the whole as “very good” (Genesis 1:26–31). The passage turns on the goodness not only of isolated creatures but of their relationships within the completed whole. Humanity’s embodied, male-and-female existence, work, fruitfulness, and dependence upon God’s provision all belong to that affirmation (Genesis 1:27–30).

In its ancient Near Eastern setting, this presents the world as God’s purposeful handiwork rather than divine debris or a cosmic accident. Its goodness remains derived, however: creation is good because God wills, orders, sustains, and approves it. Accordingly, creation directs honor back to its Creator: “by Your will they exist and were created” (Revelation 4:11).
