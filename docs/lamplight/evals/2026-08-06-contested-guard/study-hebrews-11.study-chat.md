## Reply

Hebrews 11:1 gives less a complete dictionary definition than a description of faith in action. Jamieson reads it in the setting of Hebrews’ call to perseverance: faith gives present solidity to what God has promised and conviction concerning realities not yet visible. The lexicon similarly glosses *pistis* as persuasion, conviction, and reliance. Faith is therefore not wishful thinking; it rests its hope on the character and testimony of God. That is why verse 6 identifies its core: approaching God as real and trusting that he rewards those who earnestly seek him (Heb 11:1; Heb 11:6).

The examples then unfold this description through repeated “by faith” actions. Noah acts upon a warning about “things not yet seen.” Abraham travels without knowing his destination. Sarah judges the Promiser faithful despite bodily impossibility. Moses values a future reward above Egypt’s immediate treasures. In every case, an unseen divine promise becomes weightier than visible circumstances (Heb 11:7-11; Heb 11:24-27). This accords with Paul’s language of walking “by faith, not by sight” and fixing attention on the eternal rather than merely visible world (2co 5:7; 2co 4:18).

Yet the chapter carefully prevents faith from becoming a formula for visible success. Some conquer kingdoms and escape death; others are tortured, imprisoned, and killed. Both groups are commended. The defining issue is not whether faith produces immediate deliverance, but whether a person continues to rely upon God when fulfillment remains distant (Heb 11:33-39).

Verses 13 and 39-40 supply the interpretive key: many died without receiving the promise in fullness. Faith “welcomes” God’s future from afar and orders present allegiance around it. Christian faith, then, is future hope made operative now—confidence in the Lord’s faithfulness that can obey, endure, worship, and even suffer before sight arrives (Heb 11:13; Heb 11:39-40; rom 8:24).
