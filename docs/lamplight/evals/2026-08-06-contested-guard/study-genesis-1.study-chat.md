## Reply

The repeated verdict—“And God saw that it was good”—acts like a refrain, teaching the reader how to view creation through its Maker’s eyes. “Good” here is more than visually pleasing: within the chapter, it describes a world that is ordered, fruitful, and fitted to God’s purposes. Light is distinguished from darkness, seas from land, living things reproduce “according to their kinds,” and the heavenly lights serve appointed functions (Genesis 1:4, 10, 12, 18, 21, 25).

The phrase also emphasizes divine assessment. God does not discover whether creation succeeded; the text pictures Him surveying and affirming what His word has brought about. “And it was so” stresses the effectiveness of His command, while “God saw that it was good” stresses the worth and rightness of its result. Hebrews echoes this creative authority: “the universe was formed at God’s command” (Hebrews 11:3).

The refrain builds toward a significant climax. After humanity is created in God’s image and entrusted with rule, God looks upon the whole rather than one part: “indeed, it was very good” (Genesis 1:27–31). Each part is good, but the completed, interdependent whole is *very* good. Humanity’s embodied, male-and-female existence belongs within that affirmation, as does the nonhuman world placed under human stewardship—not merely consumption (Genesis 1:26–30).

Finally, this repeated goodness resists any view that matter is inherently evil or that the world arose without intention. Isaiah similarly says the Lord formed the earth “to be inhabited,” and Revelation grounds worship in the truth that all things exist by God’s will (Isaiah 45:18; Revelation 4:11). Genesis therefore begins with gift before it ever speaks of human sin: the created order is originally God-willed, purposeful, and good.
