## Reply

David’s “one thing” is best read not merely as a wish to live inside a religious building, but as a desire to make the LORD’s presence the governing center of his whole life. The verse itself explains “dwell”: he wants “to gaze on the beauty of the LORD and seek Him in His temple” (Psalms 27:4). Dwelling therefore includes worship, contemplation, prayer, and sustained fellowship with God.

The language is poetic and devotional. Elsewhere in the psalm David speaks of God’s “shelter,” “tent,” and “tabernacle,” images of sanctuary and protection (Psalms 27:5–6). His enemies may surround him, but his deepest request is not first that the danger disappear. It is that he remain near the LORD within it. That is the striking turn in the psalm: security comes from communion with God, not simply from improved circumstances.

“Beauty” here points toward everything in the LORD that makes him worthy of adoration—his goodness, faithfulness, holiness, and saving care as displayed in the psalm. David begins by calling the LORD “my light,” “my salvation,” and “the stronghold of my life” (Psalms 27:1). Spurgeon emphasizes this personal language: David does not merely say that God *gives* light and salvation, but that the LORD *is* these things to him. Jamieson likewise understands the stronghold as security against violence.

“All the days of my life” consequently expresses an enduring orientation rather than a passing visit. David wants seeking God’s face to characterize his entire life: “Your face, O LORD, I will seek” (Psalms 27:8). In modern terms, the verse invites a question deeper than “How can I escape trouble?”: “What would it mean for the Lord himself to remain my chief desire while trouble is still present?”
