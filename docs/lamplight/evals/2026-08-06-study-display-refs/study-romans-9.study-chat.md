## Reply

Romans 9 is a major text in that debate, but orthodox Christians disagree about precisely what its election language establishes.

**Reformed readings** answer yes: Jacob and Esau are distinguished “before the twins were born or had done anything good or bad,” so that election rests “not by works but by Him who calls” (Romans 9:11–12). Paul then says mercy does not depend on human desire or effort, and describes God preparing “vessels of His mercy” for glory (Romans 9:16, 23). On this reading, Paul is teaching God’s unconditional choice of particular people for salvation.

**Wesleyan/Arminian and many corporate-election readings** emphasize Paul’s larger question: Has God’s word failed regarding Israel? (Romans 9:6). Isaac and Jacob represent the chosen line through which the promise advances, while the Hosea and Isaiah quotations concern peoples—the inclusion of Gentiles and the preservation of an Israelite remnant (Romans 9:24–29). They therefore read the chapter primarily as God’s sovereign freedom to define His covenant people in Christ, not as a complete account of each individual’s eternal destiny. They also point to Paul’s concluding distinction: righteousness is received “by faith,” while Israel stumbled by pursuing it “as if it were by works” (Romans 9:30–33).

The passage turns on both divine initiative and faith. God’s mercy cannot be earned or controlled (Romans 9:15–16), yet Paul identifies those who belong to the justified people as those who believe in Christ, the cornerstone (Romans 9:32–33; Isaiah 28:16).

So Romans 9 can support individual predestination, but whether that is its exclusive or primary teaching is genuinely disputed among orthodox Christians. The most responsible reading follows the argument through Romans 9–11 and discusses the competing interpretations with one’s pastor or study group rather than treating Romans 9 alone as settling every question.
