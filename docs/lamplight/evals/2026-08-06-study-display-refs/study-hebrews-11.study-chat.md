## Reply

Hebrews 11:1 is less a complete dictionary definition of faith than a description of how faith functions: “Now faith is the assurance of what we hope for and the certainty of what we do not see.” (Hebrews 11:1). Jamieson, Fausset & Brown reads “assurance” as either the present substance of promised realities or a sure confidence in them. The lexicon similarly describes *pistis* as persuasion, conviction, and reliance. Faith therefore treats God’s promised future as solid enough to shape present life.

The chapter’s examples unfold that definition through three movements:

**Faith receives God’s testimony about what cannot be observed.** Creation itself was not witnessed by us; nevertheless, “by faith we understand” that the visible order came at God’s command (Hebrews 11:3). Faith is not portrayed as admiration for the unexplained, but as trust in the God who speaks.

**Faith acts before fulfillment is visible.** Noah prepares for an unseen flood; Abraham travels without knowing his destination; Sarah considers the Promiser faithful; Joseph gives instructions concerning an exodus that has not yet occurred (Hebrews 11:7–11, 22). Their actions make hope concrete. This matches Paul’s language: “For we walk by faith, not by sight.” (2 Corinthians 5:7), fixing attention on the unseen and eternal rather than the visible and temporary (2 Corinthians 4:18).

**Faith perseveres even when fulfillment does not arrive within one lifetime.** The chapter deliberately refuses a simple success formula. Some conquer kingdoms and escape death; others are imprisoned, tortured, and killed (Hebrews 11:33–38). Both groups are commended. The decisive issue is not whether faith produces immediate deliverance, but whether it continues to regard God as faithful. Indeed, the patriarchs “died in faith” while welcoming the promises “from afar” (Hebrews 11:13), and all were commended without yet receiving the promise in fullness (Hebrews 11:39–40).

So the examples interpret verse 1: faith is confidence in God’s unseen reality and promised future, expressed as obedient endurance in the present—whether the present brings victory, waiting, or suffering.
