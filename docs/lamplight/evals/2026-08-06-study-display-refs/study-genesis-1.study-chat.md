## Reply

The repeated phrase functions like God’s own evaluation of creation. “Good” here is not merely “pleasant” or “morally innocent.” In the flow of Genesis 1, it means that what God has made corresponds to His purpose: light illuminates, land supports life, plants reproduce, and creatures inhabit their appointed realms. Creation is ordered, fruitful, and fit for habitation—an emphasis echoed when Isaiah says the Lord “did not create [the earth] to be empty, but formed it to be inhabited” (Isaiah 45:18).

The repetition also marks stages in a movement from disorder toward a flourishing world. The chapter begins with the earth “formless and void,” dark and watery (Genesis 1:2). Through God’s speech, distinctions appear—light and darkness, waters and sky, land and seas—and then these realms are filled. After each major stage, “God saw” confirms that the result is genuinely good in His sight, not an accident or a rival power’s work (Genesis 1:4, 10, 12, 18, 21, 25).

The climax sharpens the point. After humanity is created in God’s image and entrusted with rule, God sees the whole and calls it “very good” (Genesis 1:26–31). The goodness belongs not only to separate objects but to the integrated whole: God, humanity, other creatures, and the earth ordered in right relation. Human dominion therefore appears inside God’s favorable judgment, suggesting stewardship that preserves creation’s fruitfulness rather than rule that despises it (Genesis 1:28–30).

Finally, the refrain is theological: matter, bodies, work, fertility, and ordinary creaturely life are affirmed as gifts of the Creator. Revelation gives the answering posture—God is worthy because all things exist by His will (Revelation 4:11).
