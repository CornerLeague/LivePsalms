## Reply

Romans 9 certainly teaches that God’s electing purpose precedes human birth and works: Paul points to Jacob and Esau, saying that “before the twins were born or had done anything good or bad,” God’s purpose would stand “not by works but by Him who calls” (rom 9:11–12). He then grounds the distinction in God’s freedom to show mercy rather than in human desire or effort (rom 9:15–16).

The disputed question is **what kind of election Paul has in view**. One reading sees Jacob and Esau as individuals illustrating God’s unconditional choice of particular people for salvation. Jamieson, Fausset & Brown take the Jacob–Esau contrast as God’s “gratuitous favor,” supporting that reading. Another reading emphasizes their representative roles: Jacob and Esau stand for Israel and Edom, and Paul is explaining how God’s covenant purpose advances through chosen lines and now embraces both Jews and Gentiles (rom 9:24–26). The source Paul quotes—“Jacob I loved”—comes from Malachi’s address to the peoples descended from the brothers, which gives this corporate dimension real weight (mal 1:2).

The chapter does not permit election to be reduced merely to foreseen merit: Paul explicitly excludes works performed before the choice (rom 9:11–16). Yet it also does not end with an abstract decree detached from faith. Paul’s concluding explanation is that Gentiles received righteousness “by faith,” while Israel stumbled by pursuing it “as if it were by works”; the one who believes in Christ will not be put to shame (rom 9:30–33; isa 28:16).

So the careful answer is: **Romans 9 supports pre-birth, unconditional divine election, but whether it specifically defines an eternal selection of each individual for salvation is contested.** The passage holds God’s sovereign mercy and the necessity of faith together. Because Christians disagree on how those fit, this is worth examining with a pastor or study group alongside Romans 10–11.
