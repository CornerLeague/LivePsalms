## Reply

Hebrews 11:1 gives less a complete dictionary definition than a description of faith at work. Jamieson reads it in the setting of Hebrews’ call to perseverance: faith gives present solidity—“assurance”—to what God has promised for the future, and conviction concerning realities that remain unseen. The lexicon’s gloss for *pistis* includes persuasion, conviction, reliance, and constancy; the chapter emphasizes all four rather than mere optimism (Hebrews 11:1).

The examples then unfold that description through repeated contrasts between God’s word and visible circumstances. Noah acts on a warning about “things not yet seen”; Abraham travels without knowing his destination; Sarah considers the Promiser faithful despite barrenness; Moses values a future reward above Egypt’s immediate treasures (Hebrews 11:7–11; Hebrews 11:24–27). Faith is therefore not confidence that circumstances will improve. It is reliance on God’s character and promise strong enough to reorder present action. As Paul puts the same pattern, believers “walk by faith, not by sight” and attend to the unseen and eternal rather than the seen and temporary (2 Corinthians 5:7; 2 Corinthians 4:18).

The chapter’s second half prevents a triumphalist reading. By faith, some conquer, escape, and receive the dead back; by that same faith, others endure torture, imprisonment, displacement, and death (Hebrews 11:33–38). Different visible outcomes do not indicate different qualities of faith. Both groups entrust themselves to a “better resurrection” and a promised fulfillment beyond immediate experience (Hebrews 11:35; Hebrews 11:39–40).

Thus verses 13 and 39 interpret the whole catalogue: many died without receiving the promise in full, yet welcomed it “from afar” (Hebrews 11:13; Hebrews 11:39). Faith does not make the unseen imaginary; it treats God’s promised future as sufficiently real to shape faithfulness now. Its decisive object is not an outcome but the God judged faithful to His word (Hebrews 11:6; Hebrews 11:11).
