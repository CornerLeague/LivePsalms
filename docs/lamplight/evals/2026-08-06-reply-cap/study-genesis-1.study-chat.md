## Reply

The repetition functions like a refrain, teaching the reader how to see creation: not as divine, accidental, or morally suspect, but as the ordered work of God and therefore genuinely good.

In Genesis 1, “good” first follows acts of distinction and ordering: light is separated from darkness, waters are bounded, and dry land appears (Genesis 1:4, 10). It then follows fruitfulness and abundance—vegetation, heavenly lights, animals—each fulfilling the role God gives it (Genesis 1:12, 18, 21, 25). Goodness here therefore appears to include fitness: creation corresponds to God’s intention. The repeated “and it was so” emphasizes the efficacy of His command, while “God saw that it was good” evaluates the result (Genesis 1:9–12).

The refrain also builds toward the chapter’s climax. Individual works are called “good,” but after humanity is created in God’s image and entrusted with rule, God sees the whole and calls it “very good” (Genesis 1:26–31). The whole is more than a collection of useful parts; it is an integrated, inhabited world. This fits Isaiah’s description of the Lord forming the earth “to be inhabited,” not empty (Isaiah 45:18).

Significantly, goodness is declared by God rather than invented by human preference. Humanity receives a world whose worth precedes human use of it. Yet being made in God’s image places human dominion inside that prior verdict of goodness: rule is not permission to despise creation, but a vocation exercised within something God values (Genesis 1:27–30).

Finally, the refrain directs worship toward the Creator rather than creation. Revelation draws precisely that conclusion: God is worthy because all things exist by His will (Revelation 4:11). Genesis 1’s repeated verdict is therefore theological as well as aesthetic: what God makes displays His wise purpose, belongs to Him, and deserves to be received as His good gift.
